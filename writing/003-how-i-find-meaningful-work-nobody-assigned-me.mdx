# How I find meaningful work nobody assigned me

Over the years, I kept working on things nobody asked me to. Not because I had too much free time, but because I kept running into problems that annoyed me, and probably annoyed everyone else too.

I call them _side tasks_. Tasks that are usually unrelated to my team's scope or priorities, and that would likely never make it into anyone's backlog or get picked up during a cool-down period.

## What is a side task?

A side task usually starts with _friction_. A confusing API in a shared component, a repetitive manual step in a deployment process, a bad data structure that ripples across the codebase, or a deeper architectural problem that nobody has had the time or mandate to address.

The size doesn't matter. A side task can be a one-hour fix or a refactor that takes weeks. What defines it is that it belongs to no one. Nobody owns it, nobody is coming to fix it, and precisely because of that, it stays broken, sometimes for years.

## Why work on them?

Based on this description, you might be thinking:

> "Are those side tasks relevant at all? If neither the teams nor the company are prioritizing them, why would you invest time in them?"

Fair questions. I know they're natural because Engineering Managers and Product Managers have asked me exactly that.

After working on many side tasks, I can confidently say they were one of the most effective things I did to grow as an engineer. They made my work more visible, they built trust with my peers, and more than once they ended up having more impact than the tasks I was actually assigned.

## What they look like

Friction can take many forms:

- A poorly designed API for a highly used module
- An architectural problem that creates a series of downstream issues
- An ownership problem where no team feels responsible
- A missing process within a team or the company
- Things people often miss during a common workflow

Let me give you a couple of examples.

### Analytics boilerplate

Let's say you're working on a task where you need to bind analytics events to existing user interactions. You look around to see how other engineers do it in different products and teams, and you realize that setting up events requires a lot of ceremony and boilerplate that is basically the same everywhere. The API isn't great and every team repeats the same setup, but it works, so nobody touches it.

That's a side task. You could build a small shared abstraction that takes care of the common setup and the properties every event needs, so adding a new event becomes a single line. Your task gets simpler, and so does every similar task that comes after it, in every team.

### Reviewing code you don't own

Your team often gets pull requests to review from other teams. They are changing logic they own, but that logic lives inside a module your team maintains, so your approval is required before anything ships.

Your team becomes the bottleneck for code it doesn't own. Your time goes into reviewing things outside your scope, and the other team is blocked waiting on you.

That's a side task too. Maybe the code should live closer to the team that owns it, maybe the module needs a clearer boundary, or maybe it's as simple as updating the `CODEOWNERS` file. Either way, nobody is going to plan for it, and it keeps costing everyone a little bit every week.

## How to find them

There are two moments where I usually find them: **while working on a regular task** and **during code reviews**.

### While working on a task

When I pick up a task, I start by exploring the modules I need to change. During this exploration, I often find things I don't like or that could clearly be improved.

It really bothers me to add more code into a module that isn't well organized or that clearly has problems, especially when those problems will get in the way of what I'm building.

A rule I try to follow is to always leave a module better than I found it. I'll probably make mistakes and add things that aren't great too, so the least I can do is improve something while I'm there. Small improvements compound, and over time you end up with better software and a better product for your users.

### During code reviews

While reviewing code, I pay attention to where the author struggled or what they clearly missed. After reviewing enough code, you start noticing the same mistakes happening again and again, from different people.

That's friction too, and there are a few things you could do about it:

- Write custom lint rules to catch those mistakes automatically and keep the feedback loop fast
- Rethink the API of those modules so they handle the tricky parts by default, or add guardrails around them
- Document those parts of the codebase so everyone knows what to pay attention to

### Slowing down

The pattern here is to slow down in a few key moments and actually pay attention to the friction you feel. It's easy to ignore it and move on. Edmond Lau puts it well in _The Effective Engineer_:

> "When you're fully engaged with a bug you're testing or a new feature you're building, the last thing you want to do is to add more work."

I've felt that many times. But those moments of friction are opportunities, if you let them be.

## What you get out of it

After you do the research and start making improvements, something else starts to happen.

You end up knowing that part of the codebase better than most people. Once your changes are merged, people slowly start pointing others to you whenever a question about it comes up. You become a reference.

It also pushes you to talk with engineers from other teams, which grows your impact beyond your team's scope. That's a key factor for growing as an engineer in any organization.

And it builds trust. People start seeing you as someone practical, someone who makes everyone's life a little easier. You'll often find out that other teams were independently feeling the same pain, which is a good sign you picked the right problems.

My favorite example of this was a hackathon. When it was announced, most teams were still figuring out what to build. I already had my list. My team picked items from it, we spent the hackathon fixing problems people had been living with for a long time, and we won.

## Where to start

### Keep a list

We can't spend all our time on improvements nobody asked for. Some are tightly related to the task we're working on, and we can pick those up right away. Others are too large or need more research, and the path forward might not even be clear yet. You just know there's room to improve.

What I do in these moments is write it down. Just a bullet point in my notes with a name or a short description of what I noticed and what I'm thinking about it.

Over time the list grows, and some items keep coming back as you work on different tasks. Those get stronger every time, because you really feel the pain, and if you feel it, other engineers probably feel it too.

Same thing for code reviews. If you notice yourself giving the same suggestion over and over, add it to your list.

### Make time for it

A list is only useful if you actually work on it. So I block time in my calendar for it, two blocks a week, one on Monday and one on Friday, around 4 hours in total.

This idea isn't new. _The Effective Engineer_ also mentions Google's famous 20% time, where engineers could spend part of their week on projects outside their main responsibilities, which is how products like Gmail came to be. I'm not building the next Gmail, but the principle is the same: protected time for things nobody will ask you to do.

During those blocks, I go through my list, pick a quick win or two to build momentum, and once in a while go deeper on a bigger problem. Some weeks it doesn't happen because other priorities win, and that's fine. The calendar block is there to create the intention and the space, not to be a strict rule.

### Share it with others

Keeping the list private is where I started, but sharing it is where things got interesting.

When I find friction in code another team owns, I reach out to them. Most of the time they already know about it, they just never had the time to prioritize it, and they're happy someone is looking into it.

For improvements that aren't owned by any team, I like to keep them on a shared board that the whole company can see. Not in my team's backlog, because these aren't sprint work, they run in parallel. A shared board also makes communication async: instead of explaining the same problem again, I can just send a link. Others can find those items, add context, or even pick them up themselves.

## Final thoughts

None of this requires a special role or anyone's permission. It starts with slowing down, noticing the friction, and writing it down. The rest follows.

I'll leave you with another quote from _The Effective Engineer_ that stuck with me:

> "The next time you find yourself repeatedly going through the same motions when you're fixing a bug or iterating on a feature, pause. Take a moment to think through whether you might be able to tune and automate your testing loop. It could save you time in the long run."

Replace "testing loop" with whatever is slowing you down, and you've got your first side task.

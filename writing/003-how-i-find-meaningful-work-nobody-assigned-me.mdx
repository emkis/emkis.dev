# How I find meaningful work nobody assigned me

Over the years, I kept working on things nobody asked me to do, mostly because I kept running into problems that annoyed me, and I suspected they annoyed everyone else too.

I call them _side tasks_. They're usually unrelated to my team's scope or priorities, and they would likely never make it into anyone's backlog or get picked up during a cool-down period.

## What is a side task?

A side task usually starts with _friction_. A confusing API in a shared component, a repetitive manual step in a deployment process, a bad data structure that ripples across the codebase, or a deeper architectural problem that nobody has had the time or mandate to address.

Size doesn't matter much. A side task can be a one-hour fix or a refactor that takes weeks. What they have in common is that they belong to no one. Nobody owns them and nobody is coming to fix them, so they stay broken, sometimes for years.

## Why work on them?

Based on this description, you might be thinking:

> "Are those side tasks relevant at all? If neither the teams nor the company are prioritizing them, why would you invest time in them?"

I know these questions come up naturally, because Engineering Managers and Product Managers have asked me exactly that.

After working on many side tasks, I can say with confidence that they were one of the most effective things I did to grow as an engineer. They made my work visible outside my team and built trust with my peers.

## What they look like

Friction can take many forms:

- A poorly designed API for a highly used module
- An architectural problem that creates a series of downstream issues
- An ownership problem where no team feels responsible
- A missing process within a team or the company
- Things people often miss during a common workflow

### Analytics boilerplate

Say you're working on a task where you need to bind analytics events to existing user interactions. You look around to see how other engineers do it in different products and teams, and you realize that setting up events takes a lot of ceremony and boilerplate that is basically the same everywhere. The API isn't great and every team repeats the same setup, but it works, so nobody touches it.

You could build a small shared abstraction that takes care of the common setup and the properties every event needs, so adding a new event becomes a single line. Your task gets simpler, and so does every similar task after it, in every team.

### Reviewing code you don't own

Your team often gets pull requests to review from other teams. They are changing logic they own, but that logic lives inside a module your team maintains, so nothing ships without your approval.

That makes your team the bottleneck for code it doesn't own. You spend time reviewing things outside your scope while the other team waits on you.

Maybe the code should live closer to the team that owns it, or the module needs a clearer boundary. Sometimes updating the `CODEOWNERS` file is enough. Nobody is going to plan for any of these, and in the meantime the problem costs everyone a little bit every week.

## How to find them

I usually find them in two moments: while working on a regular task and during code reviews.

### While working on a task

When I pick up a task, I start by exploring the modules I need to change, and during that exploration I often find things I don't like or that could clearly be better.

It really bothers me to add more code to a module that isn't well organized or that clearly has problems, especially when those problems will get in the way of what I'm building.

I try to always leave a module better than I found it. I'll probably make mistakes and add things that aren't great too, so the least I can do is fix something while I'm there. Small improvements add up, and over time you end up with better software and a better product for your users.

### During code reviews

When I review code, I pay attention to where the author struggled or what they missed. After enough reviews, you start seeing the same mistakes again and again, from different people.

When that happens, a few things can help:

- Write custom lint rules that catch those mistakes automatically and keep the feedback loop fast
- Rethink the API of those modules so they handle the tricky parts by default, or add guardrails around them
- Document those parts of the codebase so everyone knows what to watch out for

### Slowing down

In both cases, I have to slow down for a moment and pay attention to the friction I'm feeling, which is easy to ignore. Edmond Lau describes the feeling well in _The Effective Engineer_:

> "When you're fully engaged with a bug you're testing or a new feature you're building, the last thing you want to do is to add more work."

I've felt that way many times during my career, but I still push past it.

## What you get out of it

Once you've done the research and merged a few improvements, you know that part of the codebase better than most people. Slowly, people start pointing others to you when a question about it comes up, and you become a reference for it.

The work also gets you talking with engineers from other teams, so your impact grows beyond your team's scope. In my experience, that matters a lot for growing as an engineer in any organization.

It builds trust too. People start seeing you as practical, as someone who makes everyone's life a little easier. You'll often find out that other teams were independently feeling the same pain, which tells you that you picked the right problems.

The best example I have is a hackathon. I already had my list ready when it started, my team picked items from it to work on, and we won.

## Where to start

### Keep a list

We can't spend all our time on improvements nobody asked for. Some are tightly related to the task at hand, and we can pick those up right away. Others are too large or need more research, and the path forward might not be clear yet, even though you know there's room to improve.

In those moments I write it down, just a bullet point in my notes with a name or a short description of what I noticed and what I'm thinking about it.

Over time the list grows, and some items keep coming back as you work on different tasks. Each time one comes back you feel the pain again, and if you feel it, other engineers probably do too.

The same goes for code reviews. If you catch yourself giving the same suggestion over and over, add it to the list.

### Make time for it

A list only helps if you work on it, so I block time in my calendar: one block on Monday and one on Friday, around 4 hours a week in total.

_The Effective Engineer_ also mentions Google's 20% time, where engineers could spend part of their week on projects outside their main responsibilities, and Gmail came out of it. My side tasks are much smaller than Gmail, but they come from the same idea of protecting time for work nobody will ask you to do.

During those blocks, I go through my list, pick a quick win or two to build momentum, and once in a while go deeper on a bigger problem. Some weeks other priorities win and it doesn't happen, and that's fine. I treat the calendar block as a reminder of the intention, and I skip it when I have to.

### Share it with others

When I find friction in code another team owns, I reach out to that team, so the people who can fix it know the problem exists.

For improvements no team owns, I like to keep them on a shared board the whole company can see. I keep them out of my team's backlog because they run in parallel with sprint work. The board also makes the conversation async: I can send a link instead of explaining the same problem again, and other people can find those items, add context, or pick them up themselves.

## Final thoughts

When something feels harder than it should, slow down long enough to notice the friction, and write it down before you move on.

I'll leave you with another quote from _The Effective Engineer_ that stuck with me:

> "The next time you find yourself repeatedly going through the same motions when you're fixing a bug or iterating on a feature, pause. Take a moment to think through whether you might be able to tune and automate your testing loop. It could save you time in the long run."

Replace "testing loop" with whatever is slowing you down, and you've got your first side task.
